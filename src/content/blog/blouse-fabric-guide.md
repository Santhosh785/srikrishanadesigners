The fabric of a blouse decides more than its colour. It decides how the neckline sits, whether the sleeves stay crisp after six hours, how much the blouse breathes in a crowded hall, and which neck and back designs are even possible. This guide compares the five fabrics we stitch most often, matches them to sarees and occasions, and explains what changes on the cutting table for each.

## Which blouse fabric should you choose?

Start with three questions:

1. **What saree is it for?** The blouse should be roughly the same weight as the saree. A heavy blouse under a light saree pulls the drape; a flimsy blouse under a heavy Kanchipuram looks thin.
2. **Where will you wear it?** Daily and office wear need comfort and easy washing. Weddings and receptions can take richer fabric and embroidery.
3. **How warm will it be?** Coimbatore is warm for most of the year, so for long daytime functions a breathable fabric, or at least a breathable lining, makes a real difference.

## How do the main blouse fabrics compare?

| Fabric | Best with | Occasion | Comfort in heat | Holds structure and embroidery | Cleaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Silk (pattu, raw silk, art silk) | Silk sarees, half-sarees, lehengas | Weddings, festivals, temple visits | Moderate; better with cotton lining | Very good | Dry clean |
| Cotton (mul, khadi, handloom) | Cotton, linen, handloom sarees | Daily, office, summer functions | Excellent | Good for light work | Hand or gentle machine wash |

| Velvet | Silk, net or plain georgette sarees as a contrast | Evening receptions, winter weddings | Poor in daytime heat | Very good; rich look | Dry clean only |

## When is silk the right blouse fabric?

Silk is the default for silk sarees and for most weddings in Tamil Nadu. Pure silk and silk-cotton blouse pieces have enough body to hold a princess cut, a high neck or a deep back without collapsing, and they take zari borders and aari work well.

What to know before stitching:

- Silk can shrink slightly and bleed colour, so we check it before cutting.
- Heavy pattu with a wide zari border needs planning, because the border cannot be eased into curves the way plain fabric can. Our [Kanchipuram silk blouse stitching tips](/blog/kanchipuram-silk-blouse-stitching-tips/) cover this in detail.
- A cotton lining makes silk far more comfortable on warm days.

Once it is made, dry clean it and store it wrapped in cotton so the zari does not tarnish.

## When is cotton better?

For daily wear, office and summer, cotton wins. It breathes, absorbs sweat, washes easily and gets softer with use. Mul cotton is light and soft; khadi and handloom cottons have more texture and hold shape better.

Cotton does shrink, so it should be pre-washed before cutting. It suits simple, clean necklines such as a round neck or [boat neck](/designs/boat-neck/), and sleeves that let air in. We go into breathable cuts in [cotton saree blouses for summer](/blog/cotton-saree-blouse-for-summer/).

## How do georgette and chiffon blouses behave?

These fabrics are soft, fluid and slippery. They look graceful but are hard to cut accurately because they shift on the table, and on their own they are sheer and cling to the body.

- They almost always need a lining, usually soft cotton or crepe.
- Seams are finished carefully so they do not fray or pull.
- Structured details like a stiff collar need interfacing.
- Light embroidery or sequins work; heavy aari work needs a supporting layer underneath.

For printed georgette sarees, a plain georgette or crepe blouse with a matching lining is a safe, easy choice.

## When should you choose net?

Net is light and airy, and is used mostly in two ways: as the whole blouse over a full lining, or as a sheer panel such as a [sheer net yoke](/designs/sheer-net-yoke/) or sheer net sleeves on a silk or raw silk blouse. Net is popular with lehengas and designer sarees for receptions.

On its own net has no strength, so the seams, hooks and neckline need a backing. The lining decides how sheer the final look is; you can choose skin-tone lining for an illusion effect or a matching colour for a fuller look.

## Is velvet a good blouse fabric for a South Indian wedding?

Velvet looks rich and photographs beautifully, which is why it is popular as a contrast blouse for evening receptions. It is also warm and heavy. For an afternoon muhurtham in a crowded hall, velvet can be uncomfortable; for an air-conditioned evening reception, it works well.

Velvet has a pile, so it must be cut in one direction, pressed only from the back, and stitched with as few seams as possible. A simple neckline such as a sweetheart neck or a high neck with elbow sleeves shows the fabric off better than many seams.

## How does fabric affect the way a blouse is stitched?

This is the part many people do not see, but it explains why the same design behaves differently in two fabrics:

- **Stretch and drape.** Soft fabrics need more ease and careful handling at the neckline, or the neck gapes after a few wears.
- **Seam allowances.** Fabrics that fray, like georgette and some silks, need wider allowances and neater finishing.
- **Padding and cups.** Firm fabrics carry padding cleanly. Thin fabrics may show the outline of cups unless lined well.
- **Lining choice.** The lining often decides comfort more than the outer fabric. Our [lining fabric guide](/blog/lining-fabric-guide/) explains cotton, satin and crepe linings.
- **Embroidery.** Heavy work needs a firm base fabric or a backing so it does not pucker.

## Which fabric is best for each occasion?

- **Office and daily:** cotton, mul cotton, handloom, light crepe.
- **Temple visits and family functions:** silk-cotton, light silk, cotton with zari border.
- **Wedding guest:** silk, raw silk, brocade.
- **Bridal muhurtham:** pure silk matching the saree, with lining, often with aari work.
- **Reception and sangeet:** net, georgette, velvet, raw silk with embellishment.
